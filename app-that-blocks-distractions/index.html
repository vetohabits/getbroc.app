An app that blocks distractions, and what that actually takes

Most of them work for a fortnight. The ones that keep working have something in common, and it is not a longer countdown.

By Shay Gavan · Published 18 August 2026

The short version: an app that blocks distractions is only as good as its unlock. If getting back in costs you a tap or a wait, you will learn to pay it. If it costs you the thing you actually meant to do, the block does something.

Three ways an app can block a distraction

Almost every distraction blocker on Android is one of three designs, and they fail in different ways.

DesignHow you get back inHow it usually fails
Timer or budgetWait for tomorrow, or tap “15 more minutes”The extension becomes automatic
Pause or breath screenWait a few secondsYou learn its length and wait it out
ScheduleWait for the window to openYou move the window, or work around it
Habit gateDo the thing you said matteredYou can still uninstall it

The first three all share a property: the price of getting back in is time, and time passes whether or not you do anything useful with it. A gate changes the currency. The price is an action, so paying it is the same as having done the thing.

What counts as a distraction is personal

The apps worth blocking are rarely the ones you would list if asked. People block TikTok and Instagram and then lose the evening to a news app, a shopping app, or a group chat. Before you set anything up, look at your actual Screen Time or Digital Wellbeing figures for the last week and block what is genuinely at the top, not what feels like it should be.

A second thing worth doing first: turn off notifications for those apps. Blocking an app while still letting it tap you on the shoulder forty times a day is fighting the same battle twice.

What a distraction blocker can honestly check

Be realistic about verification, because this is where marketing tends to get loose. An Android app can see which app is in the foreground and for how long. It can ask you to photograph something. It cannot know whether you read the page you photographed, and it cannot read another app to confirm you finished a lesson or a workout inside it.

So the honest version of a habit gate is: it checks that you spent the time, or that you produced evidence you were where you said you would be. That is a much lower bar than mind reading and a much higher one than tapping a box.

Setting one up so it survives week three

  • Block fewer apps than you want to. Three apps you never get back is better than fifteen you switch off on day four.
  • Make the unlock small. The habit you gate behind should be something you would do on a bad day, not your ideal morning.
  • Decide the rule while you are calm. The whole point is that the version of you who set it up gets a say later.
  • Do not gate anything you need in an emergency. Maps, phone, messages and banking stay open.

Broc is the gate version of this for Android: you choose the apps, set the habits that unlock them, and the apps stay shut until the habit is done.
